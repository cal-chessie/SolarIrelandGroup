## The model

**Cal owns everything and stays owning it.** The 32 county `.ie` domains, the brand, the system. Installers are licensed to operate a county, not to own it.

- Roughly **€1,000 a month** per installer for the county licence and the platform.
- Each installer runs their county site with **their own content, their own photos, their own jobs and their own reviews**.
- Each has **their own Google Business Profile**, verified at their own real premises.
- **Contracts differ per installer.** A minimum ad spend (around €500/month) is one lever; strong operators in high-demand counties will spend many times that.
- Counties are not equal. A top performer may be given the `solarirelandgroup.ie` page itself.

Two layers, and they must not compete for the same queries:

| | Targets |
|---|---|
| `solarirelandgroup.ie/counties/<county>` | County-level and national brand: "solar panels Meath", the grant, pricing, the guarantee behind it |
| The installer's county site | Town-level: "solar panel installers Navan", their crew, their jobs, their reviews, local grid quirks |

Same term on both splits the signal. Different granularity, and the installer's site earns its fee instead of duplicating the parent.

## Why it is safe, and the one thing that would break it

Cal owns all 32, so these are **not** independent parties. The "independent businesses link to each other naturally" argument does **not** apply here, and anyone reasoning from it will reach the wrong conclusion.

What actually makes this a legitimate franchise network rather than a link scheme:

1. **The content is genuinely per-installer.** Their own words, their own photographs, their own completed jobs.
2. **The reviews are genuinely per-installer**, earned on their own GBP, at their own verified address.
3. **The GBPs are real.** A real business, a real premises, a real person who verified it. This is the part a competitor cannot manufacture, and it is the whole local-authority play.

**What would break it:** 32 sites generated from one template with the county name swapped, on shared hosting with a shared analytics ID, carrying an identical sitewide footer link with identical anchor text. That converts a real network into the exact pattern Google penalises.

So: **never "standardise" the county sites into one template to save time.** The variation is not untidiness to be cleaned up. It is the thing keeping the network compliant.

Two more that follow from the same principle:

- **Never script reviews.** Telling customers what phrases to use is review manipulation, and the penalty lands on the GBP, which is the least replaceable asset in the whole structure. Ask for reviews; never write them.
- **Never use a virtual office or mailbox** for a GBP. Instant flag. The installer's real premises is the anchor, the address is hidden, and it is set up as a Service Area Business with the area kept tight to the county and its borders.

## Sequencing

**Three counties, not thirty-two.** Launch three, see what indexes and what ranks, then roll the pattern out with evidence. Thirty-two new domains appearing in a month with similar structures is the shape that trips spam detection, and doing it blind wastes the first three counties' worth of learning.
